# The RBE model

**Source:** StableLego (Liu, Deng, Wang, Liu — RA-L 2024) · `stability/model.py`,
`stability/constants.py` · formulation *proved*, constants *measured elsewhere*
{ .provenance }

Rigid-Block Equilibrium treats every brick as a rigid body and every connection as a
set of unilateral contact forces. The model's job is to build the matrix $A$ and
vector $b$ such that $AF + b$ stacks every brick's equilibrium residual.

---

## Rows: what balance means per brick

Each brick contributes 5 rows — three force and two torque:

$$
(f_x,\; f_y,\; f_z,\; \tau_x,\; \tau_y)
$$

plus an optional sixth, $\tau_z$ (yaw torque), enabled by `SolverConfig.torque_z`,
**on by default**. StableLego drops it.

Lever arms are taken about **each brick's own filled-cell centroid**:

$$
c_i = \operatorname{mean}\big(\mathrm{filled\_cells}(i)\big) + (0,0,\tfrac12)
$$

in `(stud, stud, plate)`, with the half-plate offset putting the reference at the cell
centre.

For a force of unit magnitude acting in direction $d$ at grid position $p$ on brick
$i$, the row entries are:

$$
r = \big((p_x - c_x)\,\kappa,\;\; (p_y - c_y)\,\kappa,\;\; (p_z - c_z)\,h\big)
$$

$$
\text{rows} =
\begin{bmatrix}
d_x \\ d_y \\ d_z \\
r_y d_z - r_z d_y \\
r_z d_x - r_x d_z \\
r_x d_y - r_y d_x
\end{bmatrix}
$$

with $\kappa = $ `KNOB_PITCH_M` $= 0.0078$ m and $h = $ `PLATE_HEIGHT_M` $= 0.0032$ m.
The last row is present only when `torque_z` is on.

!!! important "One expression, three consumers"

    `force_entries()` is the *single shared* implementation used by the batch
    assembler, the warm prefix solver, and the BrickSim field basis. All three
    therefore compute bit-identical floats. Duplicating this expression would be the
    easiest way to introduce a drift bug that only appears on large models.

## Variables: what forces exist

All variables are non-negative. Unilateral contact is expressed by giving each
physical interaction its own non-negative magnitude rather than by allowing signed
forces.

### Vertical stud mates

For each mated knob, with normal $(0,0,1)$:

| Variable | Per | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **normal** | contact point | Compression across the joint |
| **drag** | contact point | Friction resisting pull-out |
| **knob press** | knob × 4 directions | Horizontal shear at the knob, along $\pm x, \pm y$ |

The crucial structural property:

> The *same* column serves the upper brick (support up / drag down) and the lower
> brick (press down / pull up), with opposite signs.

Newton's third law therefore holds **by construction**, not by an added constraint.
There is no way to write a solution in which one brick pushes and its neighbour is not
pushed back.

Contact points per knob come from a fixed pattern (below), so a 2×4 brick resting on
another contributes 8 knobs × (3 or 4) points × 2 variables, plus 8 × 4 knob presses.

### Lateral (SNOT) mates

A sideways stud gets the same treatment with the geometry rotated into the **vertical
mating plane**: the contact pattern is laid out with the transverse axis in studs and
the vertical converted by the *physics* ratio
`plates_per_stud = KNOB_PITCH_M / PLATE_HEIGHT_M = 2.4375`.

The normal/drag pair acts along the stud axis, so lateral pull-off rides the same
`T`-bounded drag machinery as vertical pull-off. Four in-plane shear generators carry
the mounted part's weight.

### Side contacts

Two bricks sharing a vertical face exchange **side presses**. These carry torque,
which is what lets side-supported structures shed load the way Luo's bridges do.

Generators are placed at the face's extremes:

- at each of the two vertical extremes $z_{lo}$ and $z_{hi}+1$;
- with `torque_z` on, additionally at each transverse extreme $t_{lo}$, $t_{hi}$ —
  so 2 or 4 generators per (pair, axis).

**Why extremes suffice** — the spanning argument, *proved*:

Without $\tau_z$, a horizontal force's torque coefficient is linear in $z$ alone. Any
nonnegative distribution of force over the face is therefore reproduced, in its total
force and total torque, by a nonnegative combination of the two $z$-extremes. (This is
Luo's corner-point equivalence.)

With $\tau_z$, the coefficient is also linear in the transverse coordinate. A
nonnegative distribution over a rectangle reaches exactly the convex cone generated by
its four corners — so four generators span it.

### Ground

Layer-0 bricks get unpaired knob contacts against a virtual `GROUND_ID = -1`.

`ground_pull = False` keeps the drag variable's index but gives it **no force
entries** — the same index space, forced to zero at any optimum. That models bricks
resting loose on a table: the ground pushes but never pulls, so a top-heavy structure
may tip. It is derived from `stability.support`.

### Gravity

$$
b\big[\text{rpb}\cdot i + f_z\big] = -\big(m_i + m_i^{\text{extra}}\big)\, g
$$

Torque-free by construction, because levers are taken about each brick's own centroid.
`extra_masses` is the hook for the virtual-brick insertion press and for payload
analyses.

## Contact-point patterns

`stability/constants.py`:

$$
\mathrm{FOUR\_POINT} = \{(0,-\tfrac14),\ (-\tfrac14,0),\ (0,\tfrac14),\ (\tfrac14,0)\}
$$

$$
\mathrm{THREE\_POINT} = \{(\tfrac18,-\tfrac18),\ (-\tfrac14,0),\ (\tfrac18,\tfrac18)\}
$$

in units of knob pitch, so $\pm\tfrac14$ is $\pm 1.95$ mm. Four points form a diamond;
three form a triangle with its apex at $-\tfrac14 x$.

Two selection rules exist, and the choice between them is a physics profile switch:

| Rule | Selection |
| --- | --- |
| **release rule** | Uniform per brick — min footprint dimension 1 → four points, wider → three |
| **paper rule** (`paper_knob_rule`) | Min dim 1 → four; min dim 2 → three; min dim ≥ 3 → interior knobs four, edge knobs three |

!!! note "The paper rule is provably inert for the shipped catalog"

    No part in the catalog has a minimum footprint dimension ≥ 3, so the two rules
    agree on every shipped part. This is pinned by a test. The switch exists so that a
    catalog extension adding such a part gets the paper's treatment rather than
    silently inheriting the release's simplification.

## The three corrections

The `corrected` profile differs from `stablelego-parity` in three ways. Each was
measured before being made default.

### 1. `torque_z` — the yaw torque row

Adding $\tau_z$ makes horizontal knob presses meaningful for twist loads and switches
side contacts from two generators to four corner generators. Without it, a structure
loaded in torsion has no row that objects.

### 2. `rotate_contact_pattern`

The release keeps the asymmetric three-point triangle axis-aligned regardless of the
gripping brick's orientation. The consequence is that **the same physical structure
scores differently when built rotated 90°** — measured at 0.0792 versus 0.1080 on a
single-knob 2×4 cantilever, a 36% distortion.

Rotating the pattern with the brick's yaw restores exact rotation invariance. The A/B
found all StableLego fixture verdicts still reproduce, zero corpus verdict flips, and
no measurable cost.

### 3. `paper_knob_rule`

As above — inert today, correct for future catalog extensions.

## What comes out

```python
StabilityModel(
    brick_ids,  # ordered ids matching the row blocks
    a_matrix,  # scipy CSR, shape (rows_per_brick * n, var_count)
    b_vector,  # gravity and external loads
    contact_points,  # per-variable provenance: (below_id, above_id, position)
    bottom_drag_cols,  # per brick, the drag columns scored against T
    var_count,
    rows_per_brick,  # 5 or 6
)
```

`bottom_drag_cols` is what `_score` reads. `contact_points` carries each variable's
`(below_id, above_id)` attribution, which the reduced screen re-derives independently
and cross-checks — a count check alone cannot catch a same-shape reordering.

## Model size

For a model with $n$ bricks:

- rows: $5n$ or $6n$
- variables: dominated by mated knobs. Each contributes $2 \times |\text{pattern}|$
  normal/drag variables plus 4 knob presses, so a knob costs 10 or 12 variables.

A few hundred bricks produces a few thousand rows and tens of thousands of columns —
comfortably inside HiGHS's range, but expensive enough that solving one per
instruction step dominates the wall clock. That observation is the entire motivation
for [warm solving](warm-solving.md).

---

## What this model does not represent

Being explicit, because the guarantees downstream are only as good as this list:

- **No rigid-body dynamics.** Static equilibrium only. A structure that would wobble
  but not fall is stable here.
- **No elastic deformation.** Bricks are rigid; there is no stress distribution within
  a part.
- **No dynamic loading.** Except the deliberate virtual-mass insertion press.
- **Friction is a per-contact-point capacity `T`**, not a Coulomb cone against a
  computed normal force. A joint under high compression does not get more pull-out
  resistance.
- **No clutch-power variation.** Every stud has the same capacity regardless of wear,
  tolerance, or how many times it has been assembled.

These are the same simplifications StableLego makes, and they are why the constants
are calibrated rather than derived. The
[physics fidelity notes](../../reports/physics-fidelity-notes.md) assess each one,
including what a finite-element treatment (BrickFEM) would change.
